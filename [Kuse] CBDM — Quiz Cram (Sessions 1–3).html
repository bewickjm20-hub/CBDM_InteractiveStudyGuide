01Heuristics & biases

Session 1 · the framing lecture. Three pricing studies, two classic biases, one conclusion.

The whole first session makes one argument: your intuition about consumers is a bad measurement instrument, and the reason is that people use shortcuts that produce systematic, predictable errors. Predictable is the good news — predictable errors can be studied, priced, and exploited.

The two definitions you must reproduce exactly

Heuristic

A mental shortcut used for quick decision-making. Efficient, usually adaptive, not itself an error.

Bias

A systematic error in judgment that often results from these shortcuts. Systematic = it has a direction, so it can be predicted.
Exam wording

Heuristic = the shortcut. Bias = the reliable mistake the shortcut produces. A question that offers "random error" as an option is wrong — biases are directional.

Example 1 — Wine: when price stops signalling cost and starts signalling quality

Ask consumers "If this wine costs more, would you be more or less likely to buy it?" — they say less, and they are usually right. Then actually run it: vary the shelf price ($10 / $20 / $40) of a low-quality and a high-quality bottle and count purchases.

Gneezy, Gneezy & Lauga (2014) — what actually happened
MeasureLow-quality wineHigh-quality wine
Quantity boughtFalls monotonically: ~45 → ~20 → ~5Inverted U: ~72 at $10, peaks ~112 at $20, collapses to ~29 at $40
Liking (0–7)Falls: 3.6 → 3.3 → 2.8Rises: 4.0 → 4.6 → 4.7
% saying it "fell short" of expectationsRises sharply: 20% → 40% → 50%Flat and low: ~12% → ~10% → ~11%
ProfitFlat then down (~2.9 → 2.9 → 1.5)Peaks at $20 (~18), still strong at $40 (~10)
Two mechanisms, don't mix them up

For good wine: price is read as a quality cue, so a higher price genuinely raises experienced liking — demand peaks in the middle. Too cheap and the bottle reads as cheap.

For bad wine: price raises expectations without raising quality, so the wine disappoints more often (20% → 50% "fell short") and repeat purchase dies.

Trap

The exam loves the pair "price ↑ → demand ↑". That is not what the study shows. It shows demand rising with price only up to a point, only when quality is genuinely high, and it shows the exact opposite for low quality. Answer "it depends on whether quality supports the price".

Example 2 — Pricing gambles: the uncertainty effect

Gneezy, List & Wu (2006). Give people a 50/50 gamble (say $50 or $100) and ask what they would pay for it. Rational pricing says the value must sit between the two outcomes. It doesn't: people price the gamble below its worst certain outcome — below the sure $50.

Definition

Uncertainty effect: a risky prospect is valued less than the worst thing that can happen under it. Uncertainty itself is aversive beyond any probability weighting.

Example 3 — Extremely high prices (Fiverr natural experiment)

Fridman, Amir & Karsten (2024). On Fiverr's "Articles & Blog Posts" page one listing is visually promoted. Control promotes a $45 writer. Treatment promotes a $1,995 writer. Same page, same alternatives, one card changed.

Result: the direction flips on justifiability — if the extreme price is justifiable, purchases go down; if it is unjustifiable, purchases go up.

Why this slide exists

Three lessons, all quotable: (1) existing theory may not give a clear prediction; (2) natural experiments are extremely valuable when you cannot randomise; (3) context effects — seemingly irrelevant factors in the environment shift decisions.

Linda, and anchoring

Linda problem (Tversky & Kahneman, 1983). Linda is 31, single, outspoken, bright, philosophy major, active in anti-nuclear and social-justice causes. Which is more probable: (a) Linda is a bank teller, or (b) Linda is a bank teller and active in the feminist movement? Most people pick (b).

Name it

Conjunction fallacy. A conjunction (A and B) can never be more probable than one of its parts. The story is representative of a feminist, so the richer story feels more likely — coherence beats logic.

Anchoring bias: an initial number drags subsequent estimates toward it, even when the number is arbitrary or irrelevant. Mentioned in the deck as a companion bias to the conjunction fallacy.

Session 1 conclusion — memorize this sentence

"We are bad at knowing when our intuitions are right and when they are wrong. We need data." Experiments reveal behaviour; natural experiments exploit the world; stated preferences are not behaviour.

02Research questions & method fit

Session 1 (second half) — the vocabulary that the rest of the course is built on.

Marketing research is "the link between the firm and the market": systematically collecting, analysing and interpreting data so decision-makers can act. The pipeline is Data → Information → Action, and only thoughtful interpretation turns the middle arrow into something useful.

The three research question types

Diagnose the question type from what the manager knows
TypeState of the problemThe deck's example
ExploratoryAmbiguous — you don't know what is going on"Our sales are declining and we do not know why."
DescriptiveProblem is known; you need the who/what/how much"Who is buying our products? Who is buying competitors'?"
CausalClearly defined; you need a cause–effect answer"Will buyers purchase more in a new package?"

Exploratory work develops hunches: pilot studies, focus groups, secondary data, interviewing opinion leaders. It does not test hypotheses.

Primary vs secondary data — the definition that gets tested

Primary

Original data collected for the specific question at hand. Focus groups, interviews, surveys, experiments.

Secondary

Data collected for a different purpose than your question. Internal records, standardised data, census data.
Trap

The dividing line is purpose, not who collected it. A firm's own loyalty-card data is secondary if you're using it to answer a new question. "Company data = primary" is the standard wrong answer.

Method–question fit (this table is in the slides and it is very testable)

How appropriate is the method for this research type?
MethodExploratoryDescriptiveCausal
ObserveVerySomewhatSomewhat
SurveySomewhatVerySomewhat
ExperimentUsually notSomewhatVery

Observational toolkit

Focus groups · in-depth interviews · empathic design · click path · cameras in stores. Observation is the right choice for exploratory studies (define the problem), new product development (understand a market, find gaps, test reactions), and orientation (learn the consumer's vocabulary and vantage point).

Empathic design / design thinking is the formal version: observe real people in real situations to find what confuses them, what they hate, and where latent needs are unmet — then visualise, prototype, test, implement.

Qualitative vs quantitative

QualitativeQuantitative
Unstructured, non-statistical dataNumerical data giving descriptive and/or inferential statistics
Usually a small number of casesUsually more cases
Usually exploratory, richer understandingUsually an action-oriented answer
Trap

"Qualitative data are more time-consuming to analyse." Correct — coding open text, transcripts and field notes is slower per case than running a table of numbers. Students reverse it because quantitative sounds harder.

Observation can be passive and quantitative: eye- and mouse-tracking

Eye-tracking shows what actually gets looked at (attention ≠ stated interest). Mouse-tracking records the path and hesitation before a click, exposing conflict that a survey question would smooth over.

Classic focus group mechanics (Session 1 numbers)

  • 8–12 people, 90–120 minutes.
  • Moderator keeps the discussion open-ended but ensures the relevant topics get raised.
  • The report summarises what was said and what was left unsaid.
  • Group observation = same people, a room, a one-way mirror, behaviour rather than talk.
  • Participants come from screening interviews; usually two groups per segment; pay $75–$400 for 2 hours ("if you feed them, they will come"); avoid professional respondents.

Asch (1954) conformity: confederates seated in the first four positions give an obviously wrong answer about line length, and the real participant often conforms. Direct implication for research: in a group, a person holding a minority opinion is less likely to express it than in a one-on-one interview.

03Backward research, focus groups, interviews

Session 2 · design the study from the decision backwards, then learn what qual methods can and cannot tell you.

The single most examinable idea in Session 2 is the word backward. Don't start with "what could we measure?" Start with "what will we do when we get the answer?" A study designed forward produces interesting numbers nobody can act on.

The eight steps — and why step 1 is step 1

  1. Determine how the results will be implemented. The decision, the person, the deadline.
  2. Diagram the final report — literally sketch the tables and charts you want to hand over.
  3. Specify the analyses — what test answers each box of that report.
  4. Determine what data are needed to run those analyses.
  5. Check whether secondary data already exist.
  6. Only then collect primary data.
  7. Do the fieldwork.
  8. Analysis and report.
The exam answer

If asked "what is the first step in designing research?", the answer is determine how the results will be implemented — not "define the sample", not "write the questionnaire". Steps 2 and 3 exist to make step 1 concrete: if you cannot draw the final chart, you do not yet know what you are studying.

Active vs passive data collection

ActivePassive
The subject knows they are being studied / is asked something directly. Surveys, interviews, self-reports, census.Data captured as a by-product of something else. Scanner records, web logs, archival records, geodemographics.

The distinction matters because active collection measures what people say and passive collection measures what happened. Passive data is not automatically better — it is unprotected against construct-validity problems, because nobody designed it to measure your construct.

Focus groups: what goes wrong

A focus group is a moderated group discussion, usually 8–12 people for 90–120 minutes. It is excellent for generating language, spotting unanticipated issues and watching how opinions form socially. It is weak as a measurement instrument, for six specific reasons:

The six failure modes — learn the names, they are the exam's raw material
Failure modeWhat happens
Clever Hans effectThe horse "did arithmetic" by reading the trainer's micro-cues. Participants similarly pick up the moderator's expectations and give the answer they think is wanted — including from tone and body language.
Moderator effectsThe moderator's own identity, warmth or bias shapes what gets said, and no two moderators run the "same" group the same way.
Dominant personalitiesOne or two talkers set the agenda; quieter participants' views never surface.
Majority / minority influenceOnce a view is stated by several people, others conform (Asch). Minority opinions are systematically suppressed.
Dynamic effectsIdeas raised early anchor the rest of the session; later comments are reactions to earlier ones rather than independent views.
Observation changes behaviourPeople do not behave naturally when they know they are watched — the same reason a one-way mirror changes what happens in the room.
Trap

"Focus groups tell you what consumers actually do." False. They tell you what consumers say, in a social setting, in front of a moderator. The canonical proof is New Coke.

New Coke (1985) — the case that carries the whole lecture

Blind taste tests showed consumers preferred the new, sweeter formula to original Coke — by a clear margin, in test after test. Coca-Cola reformulated and launched it. It was a commercial catastrophe and the original was back within 79 days.

Why the tests were wrong — four reasons, all now standard:

  • Sip vs full can. Tasters had a mouthful or two. A sweeter formula wins a sip and becomes cloying over a whole can. The test measured the wrong consumption episode.
  • The wrong counterfactual. Tasters were asked "which do you prefer?" — not "should we replace the old one?" Preference between two cups is a different question from losing a product you buy every week.
  • Brand identity. Coke was "The Real Thing". The brand was continuity, not flavour. Removing the original removed the product people were loyal to.
  • Social dynamics. Once the withdrawal was announced, anger about losing the old Coke exceeded any preference for the new taste — people stockpiled, protested, and formed an identity around the grievance.
The general lesson

Taste tests measure preference in a context stripped of brand, habit, price, availability and loss. Change any of those and the answer changes. This is also the deck's warning about expectancy effects: what you tell someone before they taste it moves what they taste.

MROC (Marketing Research Online Community) is the modern successor: a standing online panel you can re-question quickly, cheaper than new recruitment, at the cost of self-selection and fatigue.

In-depth interviews

Semi-structured, one participant, guided by a guide (topics) plus a schedule (the exact prompts and their order). The right choice when the topic is sensitive, confidential, or socially embarrassing — the exact conditions where a group suppresses honest answers, per Asch.

StrengthsCosts
Depth, nuance, the reasoning behind a choice; safe space for sensitive topics; catches what a closed question cannot.Expensive per respondent; slow; small n so no statistical inference; interviewer skill dominates the quality.

The six question-writing rules for interviews

  • Open-ended, not yes/no.
  • Prompt from memory rather than showing a list — a list bounds the answer space.
  • Concrete, about a specific occasion, not "generally".
  • Leave white space — silence is a technique; the useful sentence usually arrives after the pause.
  • Avoid yes/no — it terminates the topic.
  • Avoid leading questions that contain the answer.
Laddering

Ask "why?" repeatedly down the chain: attribute → functional consequence → personal value. "Why do you buy this brand?" → "it's reliable" → "why does reliable matter?" → "so I never look unprepared at work" → "why does that matter?" → "I'd feel I was failing." That last rung is the one a single question never reaches.

This is the deck's unknown unknowns point (the Rumsfeld frame): the most valuable insight is the thing the respondent could not have told you if you had asked directly, because they had never articulated it. Laddering is how you manufacture that answer.

04Secondary data

Session 2 · everything you can learn without asking a single question, and the two validities it costs you.

Secondary data is any data collected for a different purpose than your question. Its economics are brutally good: cheaper, faster, often larger and more accurate than anything you could field yourself. Its cost is epistemic — you did not design the collection, so you cannot fully know what the numbers mean.

The taxonomy

Session 2's classification — matches the active/passive split from Module 3
ActivePassive
Census data · self-reports · surveys (collected by someone else for their own purpose)Archival records · point-of-sale / scanner data · geodemographics · emerging tech data (mobile, search, satellite)

The sources worth knowing by name

Archival records

Police reports, weather records, court filings, school enrolment. Free, reliable, long history — but recorded for administrative reasons, so the categories rarely match your construct.

Retail scanner / UPC data

Every checkout scan records product (UPC), price paid, characteristics, exact time and any coupon used. Mergeable with shelf-placement, display and advertising data — which is what makes it powerful.

Household panel data

Same households reporting purchases repeatedly: daily, longitudinal, scanner-based. Technically "active" (people do log in) but analysed as passive. Gold standard for switching and repeat-purchase questions.

Syndicated services

Commercial data sold to many clients. Their value proposition is exactly three things: completeness, timeliness, accuracy.

What scanner data is uniquely good at: promotions (including borrowing demand from future purchases — a promotion week that steals next week's sales looks like growth), display and feature effects, and separating substitutes (sales up, competitor up, category flat) from complements (sales up, partner product up, category up).

The Chile warning-label study

Araya et al. (2022) used the phased rollout of Chile's black octagon warning labels on packaged food. Result: breakfast cereal purchases fell 6.2%; chocolates and cookies showed no statistically significant change. The lesson is twofold — labels moved the category that had a plausible substitute (cereal has "healthy" alternatives; cookies don't pretend to) and, more importantly, a policy-relevant natural experiment was answerable without collecting any new data, because the purchase records already existed.

Geodemographics, and the modern passive sources

Geodemographics classifies neighbourhoods by the characteristics of the people who live there — the deck's phrase is "birds of a feather flock together". Postcode-level profiling predicts consumption, media habits and risk far better than chance, because residential sorting is real. It is also the classic ethical flashpoint: it infers individual traits from where someone lives.

  • Harari et al. (2016) — mobile phone data used to predict personality traits from usage patterns.
  • Long, Chen & Rohla (2020) — Hurricane Irma evacuation behaviour reconstructed from mobility data, aggregated by voting precinct, which exposed politically patterned evacuation.
  • Search data — revealed interest at scale, in real time, before any purchase happens.

The two validities you pay for — this is the examinable core of the module

Construct validity — threatened by proxies

You wanted a construct ("brand loyalty", "health consciousness") and you got a proxy ("repeat purchase", "cereal bought"). The proxy may correlate for reasons unrelated to the construct — people repeat-buy because of shelf position, not loyalty. And you have no knowledge of how the data were collected, so measurement error is invisible to you.

Internal validity — threatened by causation

Secondary data is observational. Cereal sales fell after labels appeared; they also fell because of price, income, seasonality, a competitor launch, or a trend already underway. Without randomisation or a credible natural experiment, you cannot rule those out — so you cannot claim cause.
The trade-off, in one table
AdvantagesDisadvantages
Cheaper and fasterNo knowledge of how the data were collected
Often more accurate (census coverage, no self-report bias)Proxy measures → weak construct validity
Historical — you can go back before your question existedHard to establish causation → weak internal validity
The only feasible route when primary collection is too costly or unethicalCategories rarely fit your question exactly; no control over what was asked
Trap

"Secondary data is less accurate than primary data." Wrong as a general claim — a national census is more accurate than your 200-person online panel. Secondary data's weakness is fit (construct validity) and causal identification (internal validity), not raw accuracy. If a question asks what secondary data threatens, answer with those two words.

05Measurement scales

Session 2 (end) into Session 3 · the smallest topic in the deck and the easiest marks on the paper.

A scale is not a formatting choice. The scale type decides which statistics are legal. Run a mean on a nominal variable and you have not made a small error — you have reported a number that means nothing.

The four scale types, and what you may do with each
TypeWhat it isPermittedCost
Categorical (nominal)Labels only, no order — brand chosen, gender, yes/noFrequencies, percentages, mode, chi-squareLeast information; weakest statistical power
OrdinalOrdered, unequal gaps — rankings, "rank 1–5"Median, percentiles, rank testsForces trade-offs; analysis gets complicated
Bi-polar continuousTwo opposite poles with a real zero point in the middle — dislike −3 … +3 likeMean, SD, t-test, regressionRequires that the midpoint is meaningful
Uni-polar continuousZero means absence of the attribute — income, temperature, hoursMean, SD, t-test, regressionCannot be negative by construction
The bi-polar / uni-polar test

Ask: does zero mean "none of it" or "the middle"? Zero hours of TV = no TV at all → uni-polar. Zero on a −3 to +3 liking scale = neither like nor dislike → bi-polar. This single question answers most exam items.

Itemized scales — the design rules

  • 4 to 11 categories. Fewer loses resolution; more than ~11 and respondents stop discriminating.
  • Label every point if you have good labels — partially-labelled scales leave respondents guessing what the middle numbers mean, and they guess differently from each other.
  • Numbers imply equal spacing and valence. "1–10" tells the respondent the steps are equally far apart and that higher is more of the thing. If that is not true of your construct, do not number it.
  • Decide deliberately about a neutral point and a "don't know". Force a side and you manufacture opinions; offer "don't know" and some respondents hide there.
Drill these four identifications until automatic

"How likely are you to buy pizza tomorrow, 0–10?" → uni-polar continuous (0 = no chance at all).
"Which is your favourite Coke — Classic / Zero / Diet / Light?" → categorical.
"Do you own a car? Yes / No" → categorical (a two-category nominal scale, not ordinal).
"How much do you like the new design? −3 (dislike) to +3 (like)" → bi-polar continuous.

Open-ended vs closed questions

The comparison the deck puts on one slide
Open-ended wins onClosed wins on
Easier to avoid leading the respondentLess pre-testing needed
Out-of-the-box answers you did not anticipateLess effort for the respondent
Discovering the vocabulary and the unknownsLower cost, easier to analyse, jogs memory with the options

Rough rule: open when you do not yet know the answer space; closed when you do and need to count it.

Trap

Students call a 1–5 "strongly disagree / strongly agree" item ordinal and then refuse to compute a mean. The deck treats adequately-labelled multi-point rating scales as continuous and permits means and t-tests. What it does not permit is a mean on a categorical variable — averaging brand codes is the error being tested.

Beyond the slides (flagged, not examinable from these three decks): Session 3's outline promises a slide on validity and reliability that the deck never delivers. The usable version: reliability = the scale gives the same answer on repeat measurement; validity = it measures the construct you claim. Reliable but invalid is a scale that consistently measures the wrong thing. If the exam asks, this is the definition to give — but check whether your lecturer covered it in class.

06Survey design

Session 3 · the biggest single topic, and the one where most of the marks live.

The governing principle of the whole session: garbage in, garbage out. A survey is an instrument, and every wording choice, every scale, every ordering decision is a measurement decision. Respondents are busy, distracted, bored, unmotivated and tired — which is why the early questions get the most attention and dropouts answered only those.

A–F: the six wording rules

Learn the fault, not just the rule — exam items are "name the flaw"
RuleThe broken example from the slides
A Simple, consistent languageAcademic vocabulary, jargon, shifting register mid-questionnaire
B Avoid leading questions"How difficult was it to get in to your business school?" — leading: it presupposes that it was difficult, so the respondent's only job is to pick a degree
C Avoid ambiguity"Are you physically able to do things like run and swim?" — ambiguous: "things like" has no boundary
D Avoid double-barrelled questions"Are you rich and famous?" and "Was the war in Afghanistan and Iraq justified?" — double-barrelled: two questions, one answer box
E Avoid double negatives"I do not feel uncomfortable…" — double negative: the respondent has to invert, and some will not
F Mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive optionsOverlapping income bands; missing "none of these"; no "prefer not to say"

Framing: identical facts, opposite decisions

Three demonstrations, all worth memorising with their numbers.

Asian disease

400 people will die. Gain frame ("200 will be saved") → choose the certain option, risk-averse. Loss frame ("400 will die") → gamble, risk-seeking. Same outcome, flipped preference.

Loaded question

"Allow" a rally vs "forbid" it: 56% / 44% allow, but when the identical policy is put as a ban, only 22% / 78%. The verb in the question sets the reference point.
Two more you will be asked to identify

Implicit reference: "Is traffic in your city getting worse?" asked traffic-first vs industry-first gives 45 / 32 versus 24 / 57 — whatever you asked about first becomes the standard of comparison.

Vibrating alarm: a feature described as costing $13.34 more vs $60.93 less changes uptake purely on the direction of the price comparison.

Series of scales — where questionnaires quietly break

  • Missing N/A. If the behaviour does not apply to some respondents, they will invent an answer rather than skip.
  • Reverse coding. Mix positively and negatively worded items to break response sets. Consequence: you must recode before analysis, or your means are nonsense. This is why "reverse coding exists" and "reverse coding must be undone" are two separate exam points.
  • Midpoint misuse. A midpoint means "neither / no opinion" only if you told respondents so; otherwise it absorbs the lazy and the indifferent alike.
Response scale effect (Schwarz et al., 1985) — the sharpest result in the session

Reporting more than 2.5 hours of TV a day: 16% on a low-frequency scale, 38% on a high-frequency scale. The behaviour did not change; the ruler did. Second half of the same finding: respondents who had just used a $0–$500 savings scale inferred they were relatively wealthy; those given a $0–$500,000 scale inferred they were poor.

Trap

Students answer "response scale effects are a form of sampling error". No — they are measurement error caused by the instrument, and they are systematic, so they bias the estimate in a predictable direction rather than adding noise.

The six-step questionnaire process

  1. Define the sample and the mode.
  2. Define the bits of information you need.
  3. Draft the questions.
  4. Decide ordering and randomisation.
  5. Hunt traps, then validate.
  6. Pretest and implement.

The backward check that governs all six: before writing anything, ask "What would I do with the answer?" If nothing changes on any answer, delete the question.

When open-ended is worth the cost

Three legitimate uses: (1) as the main dependent variable — coded with a codebook and multiple coders, or with topic modelling (Zhang, Li & Allenby, 2024); (2) to get elaboration and unknown unknowns — the things you did not know to offer as an option; (3) feedback. The costs are real: hard to analyse, hard for the respondent to answer, slow to field.

Defaults are a design decision

Organ-donor registration (Johnson & Goldstein, 2004): opt-in countries sit near 15–20%, opt-out countries near 90%+. Nobody re-expresses a preference — the default does the choosing. If your question has a pre-selected answer, you have written a policy, not a measurement.

Sensitive questions

Asking about the illegal, the embarrassing or the socially judged produces three failures: item nonresponse (skip it), termination (quit the survey), and distortion (answer what is socially acceptable). The bias runs downward on stigmatised behaviour — people under-report.

Fixes that work

Rapport first · a counter-biasing statement that normalises the behaviour ("a man often uses his wife's hair spray…") · the other-people approach — ask what "people you know" do, then infer.

The number that proves it

Netherlands cannabis use: 5.1% direct question vs 17.4% via the other-people approach — and the higher figure was corroborated by sewage testing (Gregori, De Jong & Pieters, 2023).

Ordering effects

  • Demographics go at the end. Asking identity questions first triggers stereotype threat (Shih, Pittinsky & Ambady, 1999) and contaminates every answer that follows.
  • General before specific. Strack, Martin & Schwartz (1988): asking about dating after a general life-satisfaction item correlates r = .66; asking it first gives r = −.01. Same two questions, opposite conclusion.
  • Response substitution (Gal & Rucker, 2011): a fondue set rated for "Anne" vs "Jane" is rated differently — respondents answer the question they can answer, not the one you asked.
  • Question difficulty drives the answer. "List 3 ways you are happy" vs "list 12": the easy task produces higher reported happiness, because fluency is read as evidence.

Layout, branching, and the ordering template

Keep the response options in a monotonic order (low to high). Never open with the sensitive or the boring. Put open-ended last. The deck's template: intro → most important → qualifying questions → demographics → open-ended. Branching: keep it simple, and always capture the screening variables so you know who was routed where.

Traps for bad respondents, and the pretest

  • Bots are now a normal threat in online fieldwork.
  • Entry screeners: captcha, IP screening, comprehension checks.
  • Trap questions ("select the third option") and attention checks, often with multiple versions of the instrument.
  • Pretest criteria: does each question mean what you think (meaning), can people answer it (task difficulty), do they stay engaged (interest/attention), and is there variation in the answers? And: take your own survey before fielding it.

Respondents and modes. Free respondents (customers, networks, volunteers) vs paid (ads, panels — dedicated panels vs generic pools such as MTurk). Modes: in-person, mail, phone, internet. Each swaps cost against coverage and control.

≡The cheat sheet

Read this last, tomorrow morning, and once in the corridor.

If you remember only six things

  1. Heuristic = shortcut. Bias = systematic error from it. Directional, therefore predictable.
  2. Price signals quality only when quality supports it. Good wine peaks mid-price; bad wine dies at any price rise.
  3. Question type is diagnosed from the manager's state of knowledge: ambiguous → exploratory, who/what → descriptive, cause → causal.
  4. Primary vs secondary is about purpose, not ownership.
  5. Scale type sets which statistics you may run.
  6. Most bad survey questions are bad in one of six nameable ways. Name the way.

The three-sentence summary of the course so far

Consumers use shortcuts, so their stated reasons cannot be trusted — you need data, ideally behavioural data.

How you collect that data is a design decision: start from the decision you need to make and work backwards.

Whatever you collect, the measuring instrument (scale, wording, order) is part of the finding, not a neutral pipe.
